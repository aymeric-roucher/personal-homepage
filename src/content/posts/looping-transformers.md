Why do we use "chain-of-thought", now rebranded "reasoning"?

The best explanation I've seen[^serial]: a Transformer of depth $L$ can only solve problems whose circuit complexity stays within a certain polynomial of $L$. The cap is structural: however well trained, the model cannot use more stages of circuitry than it has, and a deeper Transformer just carries a higher cap of its own.

Now, enter chain-of-thought.[^kojima] Prepend "Let's think step by step" and the decoder Large Language Model spends many more tokens on reflections before formulating its answer. At sufficient model scale, the gain sometimes reached 40 percentage points.

OpenAI's o1[^o1] industrialized the chain: reasoning tokens before the answer, no prefix needed, plus a new scaling law where performance climbs with inference-time compute. DeepSeek-R1 reproduced the method within months, delimiting the reasoning with a `<think>` token.

Why did the trick work so well? The best explanation I've found is the same paper cited above[^serial], which introduces the notion of serial complexity explained in my introduction: each generated token is one more serial step, so the reasoning chain deepens the circuit one forward pass at a time.

The diagram shows the bandwidth of information, for a model like [Qwen3.6-27B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B). In bits, the three directions carry:

- **Top-down, through the layers**: the residual stream is $5{,}120$ numbers in bfloat16, so every one of the $64$ layer-to-layer hops passes $5{,}120 \times 16 = 81{,}920$ bits per token.
- **Left-right, between tokens of the sequence**: masked attention reads past tokens through their keys and values. With grouped-query attention ($8$ key-value heads of dimension $128$), each layer stores $2 \times 8 \times 128 = 2{,}048$ numbers per token, so a past token hands $2{,}048 \times 16 \text{ bits} \times 64 \text{ layers} \approx 2.1$ million bits to every later token.
- **The loop, from one pass to the next**: one sampled token among $151{,}936$ possible ones, so $\log_2(151{,}936) \approx 17$ bits.

Can you see how reduced it is for the serial part? The loop is roughly $4{,}800\times$ narrower than a single layer hop, and $120{,}000\times$ narrower than the attention channel. This might be a huge bottleneck.

The loop is also the only edge that extends depth: an attention hop costs a layer too (layer $\ell$ reads keys and values computed at layer $\ell-1$), so a path that never loops is capped at $L$ steps however long the sequence. Depth beyond $L$ is bought only through the 17-bit funnel.

What the loop restricts is access. Under greedy decoding the whole chain is already determined by the prompt, so no Shannon information is created there. Past states do survive in the key-value cache, but a state computed at layer $\ell$ is readable only above $\ell$, so it can never re-enter the bottom of the stack, and the top layer's state is discarded once decoded. Each pass therefore rebuilds its working state from 17 bits, and must write down in words whatever it wants to keep.

Maybe this is why recurrent Transformer architectures work well. An ablation suggests itself: mask a growing part of the vector a looped Transformer passes between iterations, down to the bandwidth of one token, and watch what performance does.

## Literature review

Widening that loop already has a short, fast-moving history. Two families have formed. **Hidden-state recurrence** feeds the raw residual stream straight back as the next input embedding. **Vocabulary mixtures** first push it through the unembedding and feed back a weighted blend of token embeddings, which is a smaller channel but one the model already knows how to read.

Note that it runs *after* the unembedding: the state is squeezed through the logits, then re-expanded as a convex combination of embedding rows. So it is confined to the hull of the embedding table and carries strictly less than the raw residual stream. | [Emergence of Superposition: Unveiling the Training Dynamics of Chain of Continuous Thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23365) | Zhu et al., 2026 | theory | Superposition is learned, not merely constructible. | [SwiReasoning: Switch-Thinking in Latent and Explicit for Pareto-Superior Reasoning LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05069) | Shi et al., 2026 | switching | Switch between latent and text mode on a frozen model, using the entropy of the next-token distribution to decide when. | [Demystifying Hidden-State Recurrence: Switchable Latent Reasoning with On-Policy Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13106) | Yang et al., 2026 (Switch) | hidden-state recurrence | A pair of ordinary discrete tokens marking where the latent block starts and ends makes it trainable with reinforcement learning and open to probing. The cleanest controlled comparison in the line. | Parity with text chain-of-thought (79.3 against 80.6 on MATH-500, 89.2 against 88.6 on GSM8K) at 12 to 17% fewer visible tokens. |
| [Full-bandwidth transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08888) | Wang et al., 2026 | latent feedback | Fuse the previous pass's top-layer hidden state with the sampled token's embedding through a gated linear unit (the state carries the values, the token only gates, so reading the state is mandatory) and feed the fusion back as the next input. Token, cache and training objective all survive; overhead is two $D \times D$ matrix multiplications per token. Trained by parallel multi-pass teacher forcing; 3% of three-pass batches turn the feedback map into a stable contraction. | On 1B models: fused prefill passes give roughly 2x pretraining data efficiency; decoding with the state beats standard decoding at identical weights on all four generation benchmarks (after instruction tuning, 71.8 against 68.4 on GSM8K); shorter reasoning traces on the base model. Not yet tested: reinforcement learning, larger scales, external baselines. |
| [Recirculation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17981) | Mozer et al., 2026 | latent feedback | On a frozen off-the-shelf model, leak a small fraction ($\alpha \approx 0.15$) of the previous token's activation at one deep layer into the current token's activation at one shallow layer. The adaptive variant trains only a per-dimension gate (token to mixing vectors) on 750 documents, base weights untouched. Relative to the Full-bandwidth transformer, this generalizes the wiring (any layer pair, where Full-bandwidth fixes top-to-input) while restricting fusion and training (diagonal additive mixing in place of learned gated matrices, frozen base in place of pretraining with the channel). Its sweeps show why the free wiring matters on a frozen model: mid-to-mid pairs win and feeding layer 0 fails, since without training the top-layer and input spaces are incompatible. | Training-free: 8.5% perplexity reduction and +1.3 points on GSM8K (Gemma3). With the trained gate: 23% perplexity, matching full fine-tuning at the same tiny data budget, and GSM8K 29.3 to 35.5. Training-free *looping* on the same models buys nothing, isolating the gain to crossing the token boundary. Missing control: no plain fine-tune on the same 750 documents. |

Coconut, CODI, CoLaR and Switch are successive attempts to integrate the latent channel into training, ending with Switch under the cleanest conditions: on-policy reinforcement learning through the latent block, every baseline re-implemented on a shared Qwen3-8B base with matched data. The result is a statistical tie with text chain-of-thought (79.3 against 80.6 on MATH-500, standard error 1.8 points; 89.2 against 88.6 on GSM8K, standard error 0.9) at 17% fewer visible tokens; the paper's +25.7-point headline compares only against other latent methods, which sit 27 to 34 points below text. Telling detail: after the reinforcement-learning phase, the model invoked its latent mode on 58% of problems, down from 81%.

A structural reason for the parity: none of these methods ever trains a hidden state to be an input. The base models were pretrained without the channel, the latent positions are masked out of every loss (Coconut and Switch alike), and Switch moves only low-rank adapters. Pretraining optimizes a top-layer state for one job, predicting the next token through the unembedding; a comparatively tiny fine-tune then asks it to carry a serial channel without ever supervising it for that.

The first claimed accuracy gain, the [Full-bandwidth transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08888) (August 2026), is also the first to pretrain *with* the channel, gradients flowing across passes so states are shaped to be reusable as inputs. At 1B scale it beats matched baselines on all four of its generation benchmarks and claims about 2x data efficiency. Caveats: its training costs 28% more compute than the baselines it is compared against, which roughly halves the gains at matched compute; no error bars, and individual deltas sit within benchmark noise, so what carries the result is the consistent sign across all eight task-scale cells; authors' own baselines; no reinforcement-learning post-training. [Recirculation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17981) (last row) adds the frozen-model half of the story a week later, on Gemma3 with GSM8K as the headline benchmark:

- **29.3%**: the unmodified Gemma3 4B, their baseline.
- **30.6%**: same model, zero training. While generating, one shallow layer of each new token (layer 9 for this model) receives a small addition: 15% of the vector that one deep layer (layer 18) computed for the *previous* token. A single source-destination pair, chosen once by sweeping all pairs for perplexity; the deep result of step $t$, normally discarded, becomes part of the input of step $t+1$.
- **35.5%**: same thing, plus one small trained component. The copied vector is the model's internal representation, a few thousand numbers; instead of passing 15% of every one uniformly, a small network looks at the current token and outputs an individual percentage for each coordinate, letting useful features of the previous token's deep processing through while blocking harmful ones. Trained on only 750 documents, the model's own weights never moving, this filter matches fine-tuning the entire model on the same documents (23.0% against 21.6% perplexity reduction).
- **Control**: applying the same idea *within* one token (re-running layers on it, a looped transformer) gains nothing on these models. The benefit comes specifically from carrying state across to the next token.

The two papers reconcile into one design: Full-bandwidth with arbitrary source and destination layers *is* trained Recirculation. Each published corner of that space leaves its natural experiment to the other: train Recirculation's wiring with Full-bandwidth's parallel multi-pass scheme (Recirculation's small convex mixture is already contractive on frozen models, so the stability machinery may come free), and sweep the layer pair instead of fixing top-to-input, since the frozen-model sweeps show mid-to-mid winning precisely where training could not reconcile the spaces. Nobody has run either.

Its most useful measurement is trace length. On base models, giving the next pass access to the previous top-layer state yields markedly **shorter reasoning at equal or better accuracy**: intermediate results ride the hidden state, where the standard model had to spell them out 17 bits at a time. That quantifies the verbalization the funnel was forcing. The effect disappears after instruction tuning, which the authors attribute to tuning traces imitating fully verbalized reasoning.

What would the width be useful for? The hypothesis in the literature is superposition of search branches: sampling collapses the model's posterior over next steps to a single token, so exploring several branches costs one chain per branch, plus text to backtrack between them, while a continuous vector can carry several branches at once. The hypothesis appears in three stages, with increasing strength:

- As an interpretation, in the Coconut paper itself (Hao et al., 2024). Projecting the continuous thoughts of their trained model onto the vocabulary showed weight spread over several valid next steps rather than one, and accuracy on ProsQA stayed high with fewer thought steps than text needed. The authors describe the behavior as an implicit breadth-first search. The evidence here is probing plus a benchmark pattern.
- As an expressivity theorem, in [Reasoning by Superposition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12514) (Zhu et al., 2025). A two-layer Transformer with continuous thoughts solves directed-graph reachability in as many loops as the graph's diameter, with an explicit construction: the thought at step $c$ is the normalized sum of the embeddings of every node reachable within $c$ steps, a breadth-first-search frontier in one vector. This proves the mechanism is available to the architecture; whether training finds it is a separate question.
- As a training-dynamics theorem, in [Emergence of Superposition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23365) (Zhu et al., ICLR 2026). Gradient flow on the Coconut objective provably converges to that construction, and the paper identifies why: the continuous objective keeps the decisive attention logits bounded, so attention stays soft enough to hold several branches, whereas discrete training drives the same logits to diverge until attention goes one-hot and must pick one branch. The prediction is checked experimentally by tracking the logits of a two-layer model trained from scratch, which reaches 96.2% on a ProsQA subset.

The limit of this support: proofs and probing both live in two-layer models on graph reachability; no one has shown the mechanism inside a full-scale reasoning model. Two observations are nevertheless consistent with it. SwiReasoning's points come from deferring commitment exactly at high-entropy steps, where sampling discards a divided posterior. And MATH-500 and GSM8K are near-linear chains with one correct next step almost everywhere, so there is nothing to superpose, while reasoning-trained models already handle the occasional fork in text, one branch at a time, paying tokens for it.

The wide channel therefore does not buy depth (the loop already provides unbounded depth at 17 bits per pass); it buys breadth per serial step. Its payoff should then scale with the branching factor of the problem and stay flat with problem depth. No study varies the branching factor while holding everything else fixed; the standard benchmarks all sit at the low-branching end of the axis. Synthetic suites like iGSM, where depth and branching are dialed independently and the minimal number of solution steps is known, would test the prediction directly, along with the sharper question: at a fixed token budget, does the wide channel raise the hardest problem solvable, or only shorten the write-up?

Complementary work in the same line, training recipes that improve on Coconut without bearing on the argument here:

## Looping transformers

A third family removes the funnel instead of widening it: loop the layers themselves. Chain-of-thought buys serial depth by exiting the network and re-entering through a sampled token, paying the 17-bit toll each time. A looped Transformer iterates a block of layers on the hidden state directly, so the state carried from one serial step to the next is not one vector but the residual stream of every position at once: $T \times 5{,}120$ numbers for this article's reference model, with no unembedding anywhere in the loop. On the bandwidth axis of this article, the three designs order as: token loop, 17 bits per serial step; latent chain-of-thought, one residual vector per step; looped Transformer, the entire sequence state per step.

The family splits in two. Depth-recurrent language models (Universal Transformers, Huginn in the table above, Ouro) still generate token by token; the loop deepens each forward pass, and the 17-bit funnel between passes remains. Full-state refiners (HRM, TRM) drop tokens entirely: the model holds a candidate answer for the whole problem and refines it across iterations, so no serial step anywhere in the computation passes through a token.

| Title | Work | Family | Idea | What this buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Universal Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03819) | Dehghani et al., 2019 | depth recurrence | Tie the weights across layers and iterate a single block, with per-position adaptive halting: depth becomes a runtime choice instead of an architecture constant. | Algorithmic and length generalization on toy tasks that fixed-depth models fail; the origin of the family. |
| [Looped Transformers for Length Generalization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15647) | Fan et al., 2024 | depth recurrence | Train a small block to be iterated $n$ times on size-$n$ problems, matching loop count to problem size. | Near-perfect length generalization on algorithmic tasks (parity, addition, copying) where fixed-depth models degrade past the training length. |
| [Hierarchical Reasoning Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21734) | Wang et al., 2025 (HRM) | full-state recurrence | Two weight-tied recurrent modules at different timescales refine the hidden state of the whole problem grid, wrapped in an outer refinement loop with deep supervision. No tokens anywhere. | 27M parameters, trained from scratch on about 1,000 examples per task: 41% on ARC-AGI-1 as published, 32% on the hidden evaluation set when re-run by the ARC Prize team; 55% on Sudoku-Extreme where chain-of-thought baselines score 0%. |
| [Less is More: Recursive Reasoning with Tiny Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871) | Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025 (TRM) | full-state recurrence | Strips HRM to a single 2-layer network recursing on a latent state and a current answer. No hierarchy, no two timescales, no fixed-point mathematics. | 45% on ARC-AGI-1 and 8% on ARC-AGI-2 with 7M parameters, above HRM at a quarter of its size and above most large language models on these puzzles; 87% on Sudoku-Extreme against HRM's 55%. |
| [Scaling Latent Reasoning via Looped Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25741) | Zhu et al., 2025 (Ouro) | depth recurrence | Pretrain looped language models at scale (7.7T tokens), with an entropy-regularized objective that learns how many loops each input deserves. | 1.4B and 2.6B looped models match standard models of up to 12B on reasoning benchmarks; controlled tests attribute the gain to knowledge manipulation rather than storage capacity. |
| [HRM-Text: Efficient Pretraining Beyond Scaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20613) | Sapient Intelligence, 2026 | full-state recurrence | Moves the HRM recurrence to text generation at 1B parameters, trained on instruction-response pairs with a task-completion objective. | Claims competitive quality at roughly two orders of magnitude less compute and data than standard pretraining; published May 2026, not yet independently verified. |

The HRM result doubles as a caution about attribution. The [ARC Prize team re-ran it with ablations](https://arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis): replacing the two-timescale hierarchy with a plain Transformer of the same size changed performance little, while removing the outer refinement loop collapsed it. TRM then confirmed the ablation constructively: a single 2-layer network recursing on its own answer beats the full hierarchy at a quarter of the parameters. The active ingredient in this line is iterated refinement of a full-width state under deep supervision, which the brain-inspired two-module design merely happened to contain.

The caveat on the spectacular puzzle numbers: HRM and TRM are single-task solvers, trained from scratch on about 1,000 examples of one puzzle family, with grid inputs and exact-match outputs. What they demonstrate is serial depth per parameter, on grids. The language-scale evidence comes from the depth-recurrent branch: Huginn's benchmark scores climb as loops are added at inference, with the gains concentrated on math and code; Ouro, pretrained with loops on 7.7T tokens, matches standard models 3 to 5 times its size on reasoning benchmarks. HRM-Text is the first attempt to move full-state recurrence to text; its efficiency claims are two months old and unreplicated.

For the bottleneck argument, this family is the limit case: it grants the serial channel everything, the whole hidden state at every position, and gives up both properties of the token loop in exchange. There is no snap back to the embedding manifold between steps, which is presumably why every model in the table needs deep supervision or a halting objective to keep the iterated state stable, and there is no trace to read at all.

The same design choice exists outside language, in Meta's JEPA line. [I-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08243) (Assran et al., 2023) has no loop: mask an image block, predict its embedding from the rest; it is the objective that makes a latent space predictable enough to iterate in. [V-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08471) (Bardes et al., 2024) extends it to video, where the observable space has the opposite pathology from text: pixels carry too many bits of unpredictable, decision-irrelevant detail, so the model predicts future video in representation space and never decodes. The looping instance is V-JEPA 2-AC ([V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09985), 2025), post-trained on 62 hours of robot video: it plans by rolling itself out over embeddings, $z_{t+1} = P(z_t, a_t)$, scoring candidate action sequences by distance to a goal embedding, and runs pick-and-place on a Franka arm zero-shot. Between rollout steps the full embedding crosses; the action beside it has token-like bandwidth but is continuous, supplied by the planner rather than sampled, and carries the decision while the state rides the wide channel. Imagination stays latent, commitment is explicit: at execution only the action reaches the world, which answers in pixels that are re-encoded. The family pays the familiar price: rollouts drift over long horizons and need constant replanning, the same stability tax the table above pays with deep supervision.
