In 2025, European defense, security and resilience startups raised a record \$8.7B in venture capital, up 55% year over year, with drones the single sector winning the most public procurement.[^dsr] A handful of companies that barely existed in 2021 are now worth more than the incumbents they were built to feed. This maps that landscape and then digs into the three technical problems that decide who wins: seeing in the dark, flying without GPS, and doing both on a chip small enough to fit on an attritable airframe. Figures are third-party where I could find them; self-reported ones are flagged.

## The map

<iframe src="/assets/images/european-drone-landscape/map-europe.html" height="900" title="Interactive map of European drone startups"></iframe>

The money is grotesquely concentrated: Helsing, Quantum Systems, Stark and Harmattan alone dwarf everyone else combined. Germany, and specifically Munich, is the gravitational center. The strike and ISR reds and blues cluster in the German-French core, while the counter-drone violets and the cheap-attritable work push out to the Baltic edge, closest to the threat. And the whole thing sits on a foundation of components the map can't show, because most of them are still made in China.

One deliberate choice: I've kept the legacy primes off the map as bubbles. Airbus, Thales, Leonardo, Saab, Rheinmetall, BAE, and older specialists like France's veteran Parrot (founded 1994) or Poland's WB Group (1997), matter enormously to this story, but not as innovators. Forty-year-old defense houses are structurally incapable of iterating at the pace this domain now demands; where they show up, it's as buyers, funders and acquirers of the startups, and that's exactly how they appear here, inside the tooltips. Rheinmetall building Kraken's boats and running a joint venture with Auterion and Destinus, BAE owning Malloy, Thales buying Exail, EOS buying MARSS, Saab investing in Comand AI, Dassault anchoring Harmattan: the winning pattern is fusion, the startup's iteration speed bolted onto the prime's production scale and procurement access.

## The neo-primes

A distinct tier has emerged that the venture people call "neo-primes": vertically integrated companies building hardware, autonomy and software together, explicitly modeled on America's Anduril. Five names carry the category.

**[Helsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsing_(company))** (Munich, 2021) is the flagship and the most valuable defense startup in Europe, having raised roughly €1.37B, with a further [\$1.2B round at an ~\$18B valuation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/11/daniel-ek-backed-defense-tech-helsing-to-raise-1-2b-at-18b-valuation/) reported by the Financial Times but not officially confirmed as of mid-2026. It began as pure battlefield-AI software, the Altra reconnaissance-strike platform, the Cirra electronic-warfare stack, the Centaur autopilot that flew a Saab Gripen E in trials, and integrated aggressively downward into hardware: the 12 kg [HX-2](https://helsing.ai/hx-2) loitering munition with onboard AI targeting, the SG-1 Fathom underwater glider, the CA-1 Europa combat jet slated to fly in 2027, plus the Grob Aircraft acquisition to own its own manufacturing. Its thesis is that modern war is a software problem and the drone is just where you deploy the model.

That thesis took a public bruise in January 2026, when Bloomberg reported Russian electronic warfare had compromised HX-2 in Ukraine and that Germany and Ukraine had paused orders. Helsing disputed it, and the Bundeswehr approved a €268M HX-2 contract a month later.[^helsing-ew] It's the clearest public evidence that even the best-funded European autonomy is being beaten by the electromagnetic reality of the front, which is why the GPS-denied navigation problem below is not academic.

**Quantum Systems** (Gilching, near Munich, 2015) is the ISR counterpart, valued around \$8B after a [\$1.2B Series D in July 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/02/autonomous-defense-startup-quantum-systems.html) co-led by Blackstone, Airbus and Advent. Its Vector electric-VTOL reconnaissance drone is a fixture on the Ukrainian front (the company claims 19,000+ missions flown in 2025), and it's running the same Anduril-style roll-up Helsing is, having bought its autonomy layer (Spleenlab), its propulsion (Hacker Motor) and Nordic Unmanned's UK arm. Unlike most of this cohort it reports real, and [reportedly profitable, revenue](https://thenextweb.com/news/quantum-systems-1-2bn-series-d-8bn-valuation) on the order of €300M in 2025. Helsing and Quantum together are why Munich is Europe's number-one defense-tech hub, and why roughly 43% of all defense VC raised in Munich in 2025 went to a single company.[^munich]

**Stark** (Berlin, 2024) went from founding to a [>€3.5B valuation](https://thenextweb.com/news/stark-defence-500-million-sequoia-founders-fund-3-5-billion-valuation) in about two and a half years, on the strength of its OWE-V Virtus loitering munition and a €268M Bundeswehr contract. It's the purest expression of the "fund the factory, not the demo" logic, which deserves its own section.

**Tekever** (Lisbon, 2001) is the maritime-ISR neo-prime and Portugal's most prominent defense company, [valued above £1B](https://tech.eu/2025/05/06/tekever-becomes-the-latest-unicorn-in-europes-defencetech-industry/). Its combat-proven AR3 has flown more than 10,000 hours over Ukraine, and it illustrates the incumbent-as-partner pattern perfectly: when the UK's Royal Air Force inducted the "StormShroud" electronic-attack drone, it was a Tekever AR3 airframe carrying a Leonardo BriteStorm jamming payload, bought under a ~£19M initial order inside a wider £400M program.

**Harmattan AI** (Paris, 2024) is France's first defense unicorn, at a [\$1.4B valuation after a \$200M round](https://sifted.eu/articles/harmattan-ai-200m-series-b-unicorn) anchored by Dassault Aviation. Its DGA orders (1,000 combat drones in 2025, then 5,000 of its Sonora micro-drone in 2026) are France's largest single-type drone buys, and the Dassault tie plugs it into the Rafale and future-combat-drone roadmap.

At DSEI London in September 2025, Rheinmetall CEO Armin Papperger warned that "the UAV business could be a big bubble." Valuations across this tier price in flawless execution, and the next section shows execution isn't flawless.

## The German strike-drone program: fund the factory, not the demo

Germany's procurement shows what governments now actually reward. In November 2025 it announced loitering-munition awards worth up to €900M, roughly €300M each, split across Stark, Helsing and Rheinmetall for on the order of 12,000 one-way attack drones. On 25 February 2026 the Bundestag's budget committee approved base contracts of €268M (\$316M) each to Stark (for Virtus) and Helsing (for HX-2), about €540M split evenly, with a comparable Rheinmetall contract following, as the first tranche of a strike-drone plan that could reach roughly €9B over several years.[^germany] The drones are meant to give Germany's brigade permanently stationed in Lithuania organic precision strike from 2027.

Two details make this more than a procurement footnote. First, Germany deliberately treated three competitors, two startups and one prime, as equals, a conscious pivot toward the startups after decades of sole-sourcing to Rheinmetall. Second, and more striking: at live-fire trials in late 2025 (the UK-led Haraka Storm in Kenya, and the Bundeswehr's own at Munster) [Stark's €135k Virtus missed every target](https://www.defensenews.com/global/europe/2026/02/26/once-reluctant-germany-goes-big-on-one-way-attack-drones/), deviating 150 m or crashing, with its battery catching fire on impact, and Germany placed the order anyway. Weeks later the Bundeswehr's inspector general cited >90% hit probability for the same drone in tightly controlled tests at Altmark. Both are true: the gap between the aseptic range and the high-friction field (thermal management in the battery, a three-month hardware cycle chasing a two-week software one) is exactly the maturity gap the contract is betting will close after signature.

Helsing's [HX-2](https://helsing.ai/hx-2) (€54k, X-wing, 100 km, onboard AI targeting) ran the same arc in reverse and in public: a leaked November 2025 Bundeswehr assessment, surfaced by Bloomberg, found only 25% of HX-2s launched successfully in one Ukrainian regiment's hands, with catapult defects, missing AI tools and electronic-warfare vulnerability. Helsing disputed the figure, iterated over the air, and by June 2026 the fixed HX-2 scored [15 hits in 17 launches](https://turdef.com/article/germany-launches-9b-strike-drone-shift-led-by-startups) at the US Project Flytrap exercise in Lithuania and was back striking Russian logistics across the Sea of Azov. The German parliament, aware it was buying beta hardware, invented a governance hack for exactly this: a *Maßgabebeschluss* that caps each firm's callable orders at €1B, forcing fresh parliamentary consent (and a six-month maturity report) before the framework opens further. "Fund the factory, not the demo," with the product maturing, literally, at the customer. Whether that bet pays off is the open question hanging over every red bubble on the map.

## Where the value chain actually sits

It's tempting to think the airframe is the product. It mostly isn't. The airframe is the commoditizing part; the defensible value is migrating to the layers above and below it.

**Autonomy software** is the highest-value layer, and **Auterion** (Zurich, 2017) is its clearest European play. Founded by Lorenz Meier, who created the open-source PX4 autopilot the whole industry runs on, it sells AuterionOS (the "Android of drones," letting one operator coordinate a mixed-vendor fleet) and Skynode (an autopilot-plus-AI-mission-computer "strike kit" that converts a manually piloted drone into an autonomous one that resists jamming and tracks moving targets). The traction is real: a [\$50M Pentagon contract to put 33,000 Skynode strike kits](https://dronelife.com/2025/07/29/auterion-wins-50m-pentagon-contract-to-deliver-33000-ai%E2%80%91driven-drone-strike-kits-to-ukraine/) on Ukrainian drones, a more-than-tenfold scale-up over its prior volumes, and it is now [cash-flow positive on a ~\$100M run-rate](https://resiliencemedia.co/auterion-raises-130m-on-a-valuation-north-of-600m-to-swarm-the-drone-market/). The lesson is that whoever owns the operating system captures value across every airframe that runs it, exactly as Android did to phone makers.

**Counter-drone** is the fastest-emerging niche, and the economics dictate everything.[^cuas] The brutal arithmetic: a Patriot PAC-3 interceptor costs \$3.7-5.2M to kill a ~\$35k Shahed, a cost-exchange of 100-to-150-to-one *in the attacker's favour*, and Russia now launches more than 5,000 Shaheds in a single month. You cannot win that trade with missiles, so the whole field has pivoted to cheap kinetic kill: interceptor drones that ram or fragment the target for a few thousand dollars. The verified results are real, not vendor hype: Ukraine's interceptor drones reached a ~68% success rate against Russian drones by late 2025 (Zelensky's figure), commander-in-chief Syrskyi said they accounted for over 70% of Shahed kills over Kyiv, elite AI-automated units around Kharkiv have demonstrated 95% in favourable conditions, and in May 2026 interceptors downed more than 3,500 Russian drones in a single month. The European startups building this, Latvia's **[Origin Robotics](https://dronelife.com/2025/03/07/latvia-awards-rd-contract-to-origin-robotics-for-counter-drone-technology/)** (BLAZE, radar + AI vision, 326 km/h), Germany's **[Alpine Eagle](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/05/alpine-eagle-secures-funding-from-european-backers-for-counter-drone-tech-amid-rising-threats/)** (a Sentinel mothership dropping interceptors), Estonia's **[Frankenburg Technologies](https://tech.eu/2026/02/24/estonian-missile-defence-startup-frankenburg-technologies-raises-eur30m/)** (a sub-2 kg solid-propellant mini-missile assembled 100/day near the front), Sweden's Nordic Air Defence, are all chasing the same sub-\$10k price point that Ukraine's Vyriy ZIRKA (\$2,000) and Dark River APUS-1 (\$3,500) already hit. Belgium's €50M emergency BLAZE buy (Nov 2025) and France's DGA order at Eurosatory show demand is now urgent. Monaco's **[MARSS](https://eos-aus.com/news/acquisition-of-marss-counter-drone-c2-system-provider/)**, whose NiDAR is the sensor-fusion brain rather than the effector, was acquired by Australia's EOS, a prime buying the software rather than building it. Detection still matters and it has produced the segment's one clear commercial winner, Australia's DroneShield (2025 revenue up 276% to ~A\$217M), alongside Denmark's **[MyDefence](https://defence-industry.eu/european-counter-drone-solutions-provider-mydefence-lands-major-contract-with-u-s-army/)** and Finland's Sensofusion, all doing passive radio-frequency sensing that triangulates the drone *and* its pilot, but the harder truth is that jamming is becoming a secondary line of defence, because fibre-optic and AI-guided drones (below) ignore the radio spectrum entirely. At the top end, Israel's Iron Beam 100 kW laser went operational in December 2025 at "a few dollars a shot," and the US answer is Anduril's reusable Roadrunner-M (it flies home if it doesn't engage) and Raytheon's Coyote, but lasers still fail in fog and can only dwell on one target at a time.

Europe's own airspace exposed how far behind the defence is. Between late 2024 and early 2026 unidentified drones shut Copenhagen, Oslo and Munich airports, overflew the RAF Lakenheath and Mildenhall bases storing US nuclear weapons, and appeared over France's Île Longue submarine base; the IISS later tied several incursions to Russia's maritime "shadow fleet" launching from international waters. In one Polish incident NATO scrambled F-35s and fired AMRAAMs, spending over €1.2M to down foam decoys worth \$10k each, the cost-exchange problem playing out in peacetime, over the wrong continent.

**Components** are Europe's real weakness. The honest answer to "who supplies the parts" is still, overwhelmingly, China: roughly 75% of global lithium-ion battery production, plus the motors, propellers and FPV cameras a first-person-view drone is made of. A European-built FPV drone can cost around €20,000 against roughly €200 for the Chinese equivalent.[^fpv] That two-orders-of-magnitude gap, more than any funding round, is what stands between Europe and drone mass.

## Ground and sea: the quieter halves of "unmanned"

Aerial drones get the headlines, but the ground and naval segments are consolidating fastest, and here the primes are already circling.

On the ground, Estonia's **[Milrem Robotics](https://breakingdefense.com/2023/02/emirati-conglomerate-edge-grabs-majority-stake-in-estonian-robotics-firm/)** fields the THeMIS, the most widely deployed unmanned ground vehicle in Europe (16 countries, 8 of them NATO members), and leads the EU's iMUGS program; it's now majority-owned by the UAE's EDGE Group. Germany's **[ARX Robotics](https://www.eu-startups.com/2025/07/german-defensetech-arx-robotics-reinforces-europes-battlefield-edge-with-e42-million-for-tactical-ugvs/)** is the venture-backed challenger, with its modular Gereon vehicles, a €42M Series A, deployments across six European armies and a "software-defined" Mithra OS that retrofits legacy vehicles into networked robots. The theme is the same as in the air: the value is in the operating system and the network, not the chassis.

At sea, the consolidation is already spectacular. In July 2026 Thales agreed to [buy French underwater-drone maker **Exail**](https://www.defensenews.com/global/europe/2026/07/06/thales-to-buy-french-underwater-drone-maker-exail-in-45-billion-deal/) at an enterprise value of about €3.9B (\$4.5B), outbidding Safran, for a company doing €479M of revenue with a €1.1B order book, built on autonomous mine-hunting and inertial navigation. The UK's **[Kraken Technology Group](https://defence-blog.com/british-drone-boat-firm-raises-175m-at-1b-valuation/)** became Europe's newest defense unicorn on a \$175M Series B, and its uncrewed surface vessels are now being series-produced as "Rheinmetall Kraken GmbH" at the Blohm+Voss yard in Hamburg, while Anduril builds its larger designs for the US Navy. Italy's Fincantieri, meanwhile, spent €600M buying four undersea firms. The startups that stay independent here are the ones selling a component nobody else has, like Denmark's Copenhagen Subsea with its silent rim-driven thrusters. Everyone else is being bought.

## The Ukrainian battle-lab and the factory migration

Ukraine is simultaneously the demand, the testing ground and increasingly the supply, having gone from fewer than ten drone makers before 2022 to [more than 500](https://techukraine.org/2026/02/16/sky-high-ambitions-10-ukrainian-drone-factories-to-scale-across-europe-by-2026/) by 2026, with production climbing from ~2.2 million units in 2024 to ~4.5 million in 2025 and a 7-million target for 2026.[^ukraine] What makes it work is the procurement mechanism, not just the volume. Through the Brave1 cluster and the DOT-Chain marketplace, unit commanders buy directly: they upload strike videos to the Delta system, verified kills convert into "e-Points," and those credits are spent on the drones that actually work, collapsing delivery from months to about ten days and instantly starving any maker whose product fails under jamming. It is the most Darwinian procurement loop in the world, and it is why NATO primes with 10-to-18-year cycles (Britain's Watchkeeper is the cautionary tale) are now trying to copy the "problem statement, not spec sheet" approach.

The 2026 development to watch is the reverse flow: Ukrainian makers are opening factories inside the EU and merging into Western holdings, funded largely by the "Danish model," in which Denmark (then the Netherlands, Estonia, Canada) pays directly into Ukrainian makers' order books rather than donating old stock. [Skyeton](https://thedefender.media/en/2025/06/skyeton-production-in-eu/) builds its record-endurance Raybird in Slovakia; Ukrspecsystems is opening a UK plant; Fire Point is building in Denmark; London's Uforce merged nine Ukrainian developers into one Western-facing company; and Swarmer, a swarm-AI startup, [listed on the Nasdaq](https://techukraine.org/2026/02/16/sky-high-ambitions-10-ukrainian-drone-factories-to-scale-across-europe-by-2026/) in March 2026 and spiked ~520% on day one to a ~\$500M cap on about \$220k of revenue, a clean little emblem of how far the narrative runs ahead of the fundamentals.

Two caveats keep this honest. The famous traction numbers are mostly self-reported: Fire Point's claim to ~60% of Ukraine's long-range strikes is a company statement (and the firm was under a NABU anti-corruption probe from late 2025, which it denies), and Ukrspecsystems' "~80% of HIMARS guidance" is essentially unverifiable and ignores rival platforms and NATO intelligence. And the sovereignty is thinner than it looks: roughly 89% of Ukrainian drone makers still depend on Chinese components, the same dependency that shadows Europe.

## The commercial survivors

The defense surge overshadowed the commercial segment but didn't kill it: of the ~\$3.86B in global drone-specific funding in 2025, roughly 77% went to dual-use companies and only 23% to purely commercial ones.[^dii] Two commercial patterns survive.

**Delivery** has one genuine scaling story in **[Manna](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260310714366/en/Manna-Air-Delivery-Raises-%2450Million-Series-B-as-It-Announces-Plans-to-Expand-in-the-United-States)** (Dublin), which claims per-flight profitability and 250,000+ deliveries with an operating model of one pilot per ~20 aircraft. Its [June 2026 retreat from Ireland](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/08/autonomous-drone-delivery-startup-manna-plots-major-u-s-expansion/) is the instructive part: it pulled all Irish operations not because the drones failed but because local councils refused planning permission for its launch pads after resident complaints. The binding constraint on delivery is now social and municipal, not aeronautical, which is why Manna is redeploying to Texas and the UK. The middle-mile plays (Bulgaria's Dronamics with its 350 kg / 2,500 km Black Swan, Germany's Wingcopter on medical logistics) avoid the suburban-nuisance problem entirely by flying between depots and clinics rather than into back gardens.

**Industrial inspection and mapping** is the mature, quietly profitable end that predates the whole defense boom: Flyability's collision-tolerant caged drones for the insides of boilers and nuclear vaults, Wingtra's centimetre-accurate survey drones, Verity's GPS-free warehouse-inventory swarms flying in the dark for IKEA and Maersk, Voliro's contact-inspection drones that press ultrasonic probes against pipelines, and Dronehub's drone-in-a-box rail inspection across Deutsche Bahn's 33,000 km network. These companies are worth watching for a non-obvious reason: they solved GPS-free autonomy and precision perception for commercial reasons years before the military needed it, and that expertise (Verity's SLAM, Spleenlab's certified vision, now folded into Quantum Systems) is exactly what the defense side is now scrambling to buy.

On regulation I'll be brief, because most of the "EU is regulating drones into the ground" and "look at all this EU money" commentary is noise that doesn't change what gets built. The one place regulation genuinely shapes a product is worth naming: EASA's SORA regime made airborne safety hardware a prerequisite for flying over people, and that single rule created a real business for France's Dronavia, which sells the parachutes and flight-termination systems that let a commercial drone clear certification. When a regulation actually constrains a product, it shows up as a company; the rest is mostly press-release fodder. (The one funding mechanism with teeth is the EU's SAFE instrument's 35%-cap on non-European component content, precisely because it pushes buyers toward the European supply chain the components section is about.)

## Seeing in the dark: the twilight hole

Twilight is a real hole in drone capability, and it comes down to a drone carrying one of two kinds of camera, both of which fail at dusk.

A daylight electro-optical camera works on reflected light, so as the sun drops it loses contrast and then goes useless. The obvious fix is a thermal (long-wave infrared, 8-14 µm) camera, which ignores visible light and reads emitted heat, so it works in total darkness. But thermal has its own specific blindness: **thermal crossover**. A thermal image is really a map of temperature *differences*, and for a window around dawn and dusk the ground, vegetation, vehicles and people have all been heating or cooling through the day and briefly converge on the same temperature. The contrast collapses toward zero, targets melt into the background clutter, and the camera goes effectively blind. Ukrainian frontline reporting documents exactly this: at crossover, operators on commercial electro-optical or thermal payloads couldn't pick out surface-laid mines or camouflaged units. Add the fact that a drone is a tiny target (often under 100 pixels) against a noisy infrared background, and there is a real gap, roughly an hour twice a day, where the cheap daylight sensor is fading and the thermal sensor is washed out. Since the war is increasingly fought with cheap first-person-view (FPV) drones carrying only a daylight camera, whoever can still see through that window holds the initiative.

There are two ways to buy your way out, and they split on the SWaP-C budget (size, weight, power, cost). The hardware answer is a better sensor: **uncooled microbolometers** (vanadium-oxide or amorphous-silicon, ~30-50 mK sensitivity) are light and sip power, which is why they end up on small drones, but they are the ones that go blind at crossover; **cooled mid-wave infrared detectors** carry a cryogenic cooler, resolve a fraction of a degree at kilometres, and defeat crossover outright, but the cooler is heavy and power-hungry, so it rides only on large MALE drones. This is one of the few genuinely European strong points on the whole board: France's **Lynred** (the Sofradir spin-off in Palaiseau) is a world leader in exactly these cooled infrared detectors, alongside America's Teledyne FLIR.[^lynred]

The cheaper answer, and the one the front actually runs on, is to stack sensors and fix it in software. Skydio's X10 pairs an RGB camera with a Teledyne FLIR Boson+ thermal core and runs a YOLO-class vision model that fuses the two streams, aligning them for parallax and pulling a human silhouette or an engine's residual heat out of a scene where either sensor alone would fail. Ukraine, meanwhile, is closing the hole the way it closes everything, cheaply: domestic firms now mass-produce compact FPV thermal cameras (Odd Systems' Kurbas-256 has been quoted around \$150 at volume), and low-cost thermal interceptors are reported hitting ~68% night kill rates.[^twilight] Better low-light silicon fills the twilight band specifically: Sony's STARVIS 2 back-illuminated CMOS sees down to ~0.005 lux (moonlight) with ~2.5x the near-infrared sensitivity of the prior generation, exactly the band thermal handles worst.[^starvis] The lesson for Europe's expensive ISR platforms is uncomfortable: they tend to assume one excellent sensor, when the front rewards redundant, disposable, all-hours coverage.

## Flying without GPS: self-guidance is the answer to jamming

The single most important technical fact of this war is that the radio spectrum is contested. Russian electronic warfare jams GPS and severs the pilot's video link, and a drone that depends on either dies on approach. Helsing's HX-2 reportedly learned this the hard way. So the decisive capability is a drone that finishes the job with no help from the outside world, and there are three layers to it.

- **Knowing where you are without GPS.** The naive methods drift. Optical flow and visual-inertial odometry track motion by watching the ground and fusing it with the inertial unit, but with no "loop closure" the error accumulates over kilometres of unfamiliar territory, and full SLAM's memory and compute blow up with distance, so neither survives a long ingress on a tiny processor. The state of the art is **absolute visual geolocalization**: align the live camera view against a satellite map pre-loaded into the drone, with a neural net trained to ignore lighting, season and even the switch to an infrared camera, and read off a position good to ~20 metres with no GPS at all. Ukraine's **Twist Robotics** (its OSCAR module, refined over ~500,000 km of flights) and the US firm **Oksi** (OMNInav) do exactly this; Greece's **Delian Alliance** built the same idea into a navigation module (Osiris) it says has logged 3,000+ km over Ukraine at under 20 m error. This is also the "GPS-denied visual navigation" that made Zurich's **Daedalean** worth CHF 180M to Destinus, and that Quantum Systems builds into the Vector.
- **Hitting the target without a link.** AI terminal guidance locks onto the target visually, then flies the last stretch autonomously: "fire-and-forget." The drone can be jammed completely in the final seconds and still connect. Reconnaissance is going the same way, Ukraine's Saker Scout reportedly classifies dozens of types of Russian armour on its own and feeds coordinates straight into the DELTA command system.
- **Doing the above cheaply.** This is where Ukraine is ahead of everyone.

Ukraine's **[The Fourth Law](https://thefourthlaw.ai/blog/vyriy-pro-10-drones-with-terminal-guidance-system-tfl-1-now-available-on-dot-chain-by-the-defense-procurement-agency-and-brava1-market-with-e-points/)** and **Vyriy** mass-produce a machine-vision terminal-guidance module, [TFL-1](https://www.kyivpost.com/post/60152), that takes over the final 400 to 500 metres and is immune to jamming once locked. A Vyriy drone with TFL-1 costs about \$448, only ~10% more than a dumb FPV, and reporting describes strike success [jumping from ~20% to ~80%](https://thedefensepost.com/2025/11/19/ai-upgrade-ukrainian-drones/) after fitting AI vision; the same logic drives the cheap autonomous interceptors now hunting Shaheds. The neo-primes sell the premium version of the same idea, Auterion's [Skynode strike kit](https://dronelife.com/2025/07/29/auterion-wins-50m-pentagon-contract-to-deliver-33000-ai%E2%80%91driven-drone-strike-kits-to-ukraine/) tracks moving targets out to a kilometre, Helsing's HX-2 does onboard AI targeting, Stark's Virtus pairs GNSS-denied navigation with visual terminal guidance, but the lesson from the front is that the cheap version wins. Autonomy here is not a luxury; it is the counter to electronic warfare, and the cheapest adequate autonomy beats the most sophisticated.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, AI vision is not magic: front-line reports say it works beautifully on flat open terrain but struggles to discriminate targets in forests, hills and urban rubble, analog FPV cameras lack the resolution to hold a lock past ~500 m, and Russia now fields multispectral decoys and camouflage tuned to fool the bounding-box, so [Forbes reported](https://www.forbes.com/sites/davidhambling/) that human pilots still beat the algorithms against well-hidden targets. Second, the most robust answer to jamming isn't software at all, it's a wire: **fibre-optic FPV drones** unspool a hair-thin glass filament in flight, are physically immune to jamming, spoofing and RF detection, and deliver latency-free HD video. Ukrainian and Russian units report them hitting 50%+ against 20-30% for radio FPVs in contested spectrum, and because they emit nothing they enable ground-ambush tactics (land, wait low-power up to a day, strike a passing vehicle in seconds). The price is range (the coil caps them at 10-20 km), fragility (the cable snags on trees and power lines), and single use (you can't rewind it). Physics, in the end, is the most reliable jam-proofing anyone has found.

## The chip that has to fit: embedded AI on a power budget

All of that autonomy has to run on a computer that fits on the airframe and inside its power budget, which on a small drone means a few hundred grams and single-digit-to-low-tens of watts, shared with everything else. Engineers call the constraint SWaP-C (size, weight, power, cost), and it bites harder than it sounds: a stealthy micro-drone can't carry a noisy fan, so it cools passively through the airframe, and a chip that throttles under thermal load can slow its vision loop enough to crash the drone. This is the most under-discussed constraint in the whole field, and it is where the dependencies get uncomfortable.

The market splits on two chip philosophies. NVIDIA's Jetson line is **Von Neumann** (a flexible GPU running the full CUDA/TensorRT stack, effectively a mini-server that can do vision, SLAM and a small language model at once) but pays for that flexibility in power, because it shuttles data to and from external memory. The challengers are **dataflow** NPUs like Israel's Hailo, which pin the model's weights onto the compute cores and skip the memory traffic, trading software flexibility for radical efficiency. Teardowns and regulatory filings let you map the actual chips to actual drones:[^chips]

Two things jump out. First, the cheapest workhorses win: a \$448 Vyriy-TFL-1 attritable FPV runs its vision on a \$60-100 Rockchip RK3588, the same Chinese chip found inside captured Russian drones, and hits a target as reliably as a Quantum Vector carrying two \$249 Jetsons. The metric that matters on a power-starved airframe is TOPS-per-watt, not raw TOPS: Hailo delivers ~16 TOPS/W against the Orin Nano's ~3-5, which is why dedicated accelerators keep appearing on the cheapest drones. Auterion's whole business is the \$15 NXP-based Skynode S; Russia's Lancet still flies a 2017-era Jetson TX2 alongside a Xilinx FPGA and a flight controller not much newer than a Game Boy's, a deliberately archaic, sanctions-proof "Frankenstein" bill of materials sourced through Chinese intermediaries.

Second, the interesting engineering is in how you *contain* an unreliable AI. Helsing's HX-2, per a mid-2026 technical paper, runs its probabilistic AI on top of an FPGA "semantic coordination" layer plus analog guard channels that will physically cut the motor signal in under 100 microseconds if a safety limit is breached, so the neural network can misbehave without crashing the drone. That deterministic-hardware-under-probabilistic-software pattern is exactly what Stark's Virtus lacked when it flew into a forest.

Here is the uncomfortable part for European sovereignty. The brains of Western drone autonomy are overwhelmingly NVIDIA, an American company, and the cheap boards are frequently assembled in China. Europe has exactly one serious contender: **Axelera AI**, a Netherlands company spun out of imec in 2021, whose Metis processor does 214 TOPS at around 6 watts (~15 TOPS/W) and is already going into "binoculars, visors, drones and vehicles"; it took €61.6M of EuroHPC money in March 2025 and is building a bigger chiplet for 2028.[^axelera] But the deepest chokepoint is one level down: almost every advanced chip here, NVIDIA's (Samsung 8nm), Axelera's (TSMC N12), Hailo's, is fabricated at a handful of Asian fabs in Taiwan and Korea. So a "sovereign European" strike drone can easily be a European airframe running European autonomy software on an American-designed AI chip fabricated in Asia and integrated on a Chinese-assembled board. Of all the dependencies on this map, the silicon is the deepest and the least discussed. (One horizon worth watching: startups like the US-based Architect Labs, which raised \$24M in 2026 to use AI to design defense-specific ASICs, aiming to collapse the chip-design cycle that keeps everyone renting NVIDIA's.)

## The global board

Europe's wave doesn't stand alone. This second map is the world: drone makers coloured by region and sized by valuation, with the components and chips everyone depends on marked as amber diamonds. Two powers dominate the picture, and a scatter of others (Türkiye's Baykar, Israel's Elbit and UVision, the UAE's EDGE, Ukraine's frontline shops) fill the gaps.

**The United States is about scale.** [Anduril](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2025-euro-defense-startups/) alone, at a \$61B valuation on ~\$2.2B of 2025 revenue and a reported \$20B ten-year contract to run the US Army on its Lattice software, is worth more than every European defense-drone startup combined; it's flanked by [Shield AI (\$12.7B)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/26/defense-startup-shield-ai-lands-12-7b-valuation-up-140-after-u-s-air-force-deal/), Saronic (\$9.25B, the naval-drone play at nine times Kraken's valuation), Zipline (\$7.6B in delivery), Skydio (\$4.4B) and a deep bench of the merely-large like Red Cat and the wind-and-solar sea drones of Saildrone. Since 2019 the US has captured roughly 85% of all NATO defense-tech venture funding. But scale isn't the same as fit: AeroVironment's Switchblade 300, a decade of Pentagon R&D at ~\$53,000 a unit (a quarter of it pure bureaucratic overhead, ITAR, audit compliance, Buy-American paperwork), proved too weak and too jammable in Ukraine and was largely abandoned for locally-made FPVs, 120 of which cost the same, proof that even America's mass has a cost-exchange problem. Just as important, and mapped here as diamonds, the US makes the parts the whole West's drones think and see with: NVIDIA's AI brains, Teledyne FLIR's thermal eyes, Silvus's jam-resistant mesh radios. Europe's answer can't be scale, which it will not win; it is sovereignty, AI defense built in Europe and free of US export controls and ITAR, which is a real differentiator precisely because an ally's chips and software can be switched off by a change in Washington. Washington has shown it will use that lever: in December 2025 the FCC moved to bar new DJI and Autel equipment authorizations, and the Pentagon's "Blue UAS" list already forces a non-Chinese bill of materials on any drone it buys, a regulation that genuinely reshapes products by dictating which motors, radios and chips are allowed inside.

**China is about the chokehold.** DJI still holds something like 70-80% of the global commercial drone market and accounted for 83.5% of all drone detections in one 2025 counter-drone dataset, which is precisely why NDAA-compliant, non-Chinese makers (Parrot, Skydio, Quantum) command a premium; behind DJI, state primes CASC and CAIG export MALE drones like the CH-5 and Wing Loong II at roughly half the price of a US Reaper. But the deeper point is components, the amber diamonds: batteries (China makes ~75% of the world's lithium-ion cells), motors (T-Motor, Hobbywing), propellers (Gemfan), FPV cameras (Caddx, RunCam), and increasingly the AI chips too (Rockchip, Horizon Robotics). And this is the one place a regulation genuinely constrains products, except it's China's regulation, not the EU's: from September 2024 Beijing began restricting exports of drones and dual-use parts, motors, batteries, magnets, flight controllers, cameras, plus infrared imagers and inertial navigation units, to Western buyers while supply to Russia continued.[^china] Both superpowers, in other words, now weaponize the product rules themselves. That is the real reason a European-built FPV can cost €20,000 against €200 for the Chinese equivalent, and every red and blue bubble on the first map is, to some degree, built on top of these diamonds.

## Is it a bubble?

Global defence-tech venture funding hit [\$49B in 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2025-euro-defense-startups/), roughly double 2024, and that is where the question gets sharp.[^bubble] Split the field in two. On one side sit companies with real fundamentals: **Quantum Systems** (~€300M revenue in 2025, profitable, double-digit EBITDA margins), **Tekever** (~\$117M, profitable), Turkey's self-funded **Baykar** (\$2.5B revenue, 88% of it exports, never took a venture round), Poland's **WB Group** (~\$750M revenue, ~PLN 679M net profit) and Israel's **Elbit** (\$7.9B revenue, a \$28B backlog, trading at a sober ~4.7x). On the other side sit valuations pricing in flawless execution: **Anduril** at ~28x revenue, **Shield AI** at ~42x, **Saronic** at ~46x, and **Helsing**, whose ~\$18B valuation sits on last-audited (2023) revenue of about \$10M, a multiple in the hundreds. Legacy primes, for reference, trade at 1.6-2.7x revenue.

The bear case, voiced most bluntly by Rheinmetall's Papperger ("what is Ukraine's innovation? That's playing with Legos"), is that drone hardware commoditizes, software margins collapse with it, and government procurement never buys like a software subscription. A correction wouldn't kill the technology; it would compress the multiples (wiping out 50-80% of paper value), leave the firms that raised billions for "resilience factories" carrying crushing fixed costs, and let cash-rich primes buy the survivors' IP cheaply, the same consolidation-by-acquisition already visible in Exail, Kraken and MARSS. Rheinmetall's own stock is already off about a third from its late-2025 peak. The tell to watch is conversion: whether framework agreements like Germany's actually turn into recognised revenue, or stall at the €1B parliamentary cap.

## What it adds up to

Europe has, in three years, built a genuine drone industry with real unicorns, combat-proven products and a defensible sovereignty thesis. But the map has three shadows over it. The valuations price in flawless execution that the failed Virtus trials suggest isn't there yet. The autonomy that counters jamming runs on American and Israeli silicon Europe doesn't make. And the mass that a real war demands runs on Chinese components Europe can't yet match on price. The companies that matter most over the next few years may not be the biggest red bubbles but the ones quietly working on the shadows: the Axeleras on the chip, the Orqas and Vyriys on the components, and whoever finally makes a European drone as cheap as a Chinese one.
